## Focus: the Modified Conventional Chronology response to the Low Chronology — 2026-05-28

### Claims relevant to this focus

1. The *Modified Conventional Chronology* (MCC) accommodates most of the 14C evidence by adjusting phase boundary priors rather than shifting the absolute chronology of the *Iron I/IIA* transition (pp. 106–108).
2. Under MCC, the *Iron IIA* onset falls in the late 10th c. BCE — narrower than Finkelstein & Piasetzky's 925 ± 20 BCE but still distinguishable; the gap with the *Low Chronology* is about 25–35 years, not the ~50–75 years originally implied (pp. 108–110).
3. The methodological disagreement is at the level of phase modelling (informative vs. uniform priors), not at the level of the raw 14C measurements (p. 110).
4. The disagreement is significantly narrower than the polemical tone of the literature suggests; both positions are now defensible interpretations of largely overlapping data (p. 111).

### Direct quotes (supporting the above)

> "The gap between the so-called 'Low' and 'Modified Conventional' chronologies is now considerably narrower than the polemical tenor of the literature would suggest — perhaps 25–35 years, not the 50–75 years originally implied." (p. 111)

> "The disagreement is no longer about whether the data exist but about which framework — uniform or informative priors, contested or accepted phase assignments — best fits them." (p. 110)

### Boundary: what this source does NOT address (within this focus)

The paper is a position statement, not a primary 14C re-analysis — Mazar reports the MCC posterior estimates but does not include the underlying OxCal input files or raw measurement tables. For the data, see the publications it cites (notably Sharon et al. 2007 and Mazar et al. 2005). It also does not engage the central Negev fortress sub-question directly; Cohen 1979 is not cited.

## Other content in this source

The article situates the chronology debate within the broader history of Levantine archaeology (pp. 105–106), and includes a one-page response to a critique by Coldstream (p. 110) which is not relevant to the central Negev question. A re-ingest focused on the historiography of the debate as a sociological phenomenon would surface those sections.
